QUICK START

Think like a wind-tunnel tester.

A wind tunnel moves air past an object so we can study the flow around it. Here, you can try the idea in a simplified digital model.

  1. Choose a shape. Watch how it splits the flow and what happens behind it.
  2. Move the object. Choose Move and drag the shape with a mouse or finger. Release to restart the flow. With the canvas focused, use arrow keys or Shift + arrows for larger moves. Drawn barriers move together.
  3. Change one thing. Try speed, viscosity or angle. Speed changes blend into the existing flow. Reset the flow for a fresh comparison.
  4. Follow the particles. Each dot follows the local flow. The dots are markers, not air molecules.
  5. Use the probe. Click the canvas to measure. While the probe is active, keyboard arrows move it; hold Shift for larger moves.
  6. Capture and compare. Save two views on the page. Use the same settings and similar elapsed steps for a fair comparison.

Beginner shows essential controls; Advanced reveals probes, drawing tools, viscosity and more. The quick demo starts with a block, then a streamlined shape. Touchscreen devices may use fewer tracer particles for responsiveness.

Simulation engine: Auto tries WebGPU first, then Rust/WebAssembly, then JavaScript if needed. WebGPU accelerates the evolving flow. Advanced users can enable the experimental GPU force arrow. It reports relative model drag/lift using extra GPU passes, which may affect FPS. Rust/WASM remains available for comparison; values are not newtons. The measured FPS and simulation steps/s help you compare real classroom performance. Changing the engine reloads the page and clears the current experiment. When working with a class, keep the engine and grid resolution the same for comparisons.

Simulation detail: Auto selects a lighter grid on touchscreen devices. Fast uses 168 × 72 cells; Detailed uses 240 × 104 cells. The two modes are qualitative, not physically calibrated or numerically identical. Changing the mode reloads the simulation and clears snapshots. For a smoother phone experience, use Fast with Normal animation pace.

Super fast advances the simulation more quickly without increasing flow speed. Its actual pace depends on your device.

A wake is the disturbed region behind a shape. Swirls are rotating regions of flow. They may become clearer after several thousand simulation steps.

Keyboard: Space = pause/play, R = reset (when no control is focused).
ABOUT THE MODEL

A small model. Useful questions.

This site uses an independently written D2Q9 lattice-Boltzmann solver with regularized BGK collisions and population positivity limiting. Regularization reduces numerical noise; limiting adds local damping only when needed to keep the populations positive. It models a two-dimensional fluid passing around stationary barriers. The car, bird and Pikachu presets are stylized 2D obstacle silhouettes. Moving repositions a stationary obstacle and restarts the flow; it does not simulate a moving body.

The top and bottom are far-field flow boundaries, rather than physical tunnel walls. The outlet approximates free outflow. The inlet gradually approaches your selected speed. Each simulation step is resolved with two smaller numerical time steps; velocity and viscosity are converted consistently to preserve their ratio. Speed, viscosity and force use simulation units, and density is relative to a reference value of 1, with no mapping to metres, seconds, pascals or newtons.

Speed colours show local flow speed on the same 0–0.400 scale for every setting, so captures remain comparable. Values at or above 0.400 share the top colour. Swirl colours show opposite directions of rotation. Density shows small changes around a reference density of 1; it is not a calibrated pressure measurement. Force arrows show the averaged model force on all barriers.

The higher speed range is exploratory: density changes and numerical damping become more significant. Use the model to make predictions and compare observations. It does not reproduce full 3D turbulence, realistic smoke, a moving vehicle, or a calibrated aerodynamic test.
